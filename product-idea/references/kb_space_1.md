# Customer Problem Space — Coaching Guide

> *Independent, paraphrased study reference. This is not official course content. The underlying Customer Problem Space methodology is credited to Jim Berardone's product-management teaching (CMU 17-619, Product Management Essentials I); this guide is a study restatement in our own words, with all worked examples either drawn from public products or fully anonymized.*

> **What this space is.** The Customer Problem Space is the first of the four Spaces of the Product Idea Workbook (Customer Problem → Market → Solution → Customer Value). It is where you define, deeply and measurably, *whose* problem you are solving, *what* the problem is, *why* it happens, *how big* it is, and *how to communicate it* — all **before** conceiving any solution. The governing course principle is: **"Build the right product before you build the product right."** The #1 reason new products fail (35–49% failure rate) is **"No Market Need"** — the product did not solve a problem enough customers cared about. This space exists to prevent that.

The Customer Problem Space is filled in this fixed order (it is a numbered checklist in the workbook, and each artifact feeds the next):

1. **Fertile Land** → 2. **Actor** → 3. **Job To Be Done (JTBD)** → 4. **Outcomes** (Actual + Desired) → 5. **Problem** (Gap + Causes) → 6. **Customer Journey Map** → 7. **Use Cases** → 8. **Problem Sizing** → 9. **Problem Category** → 10. **Problem Statement**

Three "levels" run through the whole space: a **Brief** level (for communication — Actor, JTBD, Problem, Problem Size, Use Cases) and a **Detailed** level (for deep understanding — everything). The guiding drivers throughout: be **SPECIFIC, CLEAR, and ACTIONABLE** — "specific actionable information, not generalities; clarity, not ambiguity."

A recurring meta-rule: **the whole space is interdependent and iterative.** Reframing the JTBD (up or down a level of abstraction) changes the outcomes, which changes the problem, the causes, the journey, and eventually the solution. Expect to loop back and revise. "It's never linear. There's lot of change."

## 0. Customer Concept (foundation — actor roles & market sidedness)

### (a) Definition
A **customer** is *an entity that acquires and uses a product.* They may acquire it for free or for payment, and they hold **legal ownership or a license** to use it. A customer can take several forms: **individual, family, group, or organization.**

In product management you must consider a **broader Customer Concept** than "the buyer." An **Actor** (a person, never an organization) fills one or more **Roles** in the acquisition and use of a product. Roles, defined by **Webster and Wind (1972)**, describe *the actions taken by people in the buying and use decisions.*

The six buying/use **Roles**:

| Role | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Initiator** | First identifies the need to buy a product/service to solve a problem. |
| **User** | Consumes or uses the product. Can be more than one group; can be **primary** and **secondary** users. |
| **Decider** | Approves all or any part of the buying decision — whether to buy, what, how, where. Can be a committee. |
| **Buyer** (a.k.a. Payor) | Holds formal authority to pay, select the supplier, and negotiate the contract. |
| **Influencer** | Offers opinions that influence others' purchasing behaviors. |
| **Gatekeeper** | Can say "no" and stop the purchase/use, OR controls information/access to the Decider. |

### (b) Rules & constraints
- **An Actor must be a person, not an organization or department.** People become users; people become buyers/deciders.
- **One or more Actors** can be involved in acquiring/using a product.
- **Simple (usually B2C):** ONE Actor fills many roles (e.g., a shopper is Initiator + Decider + Buyer + User at once).
- **Complex (usually B2B):** MANY Actors fill the roles, and some may be **external** to the company.
- **There can be more than one person per role, and more than one role per person.**
- **2-sided markets (a.k.a. platforms, marketplaces, exchanges):** some situations have **two distinct Customer Concepts** and **distinct product offerings for each side.**

### (c) Templates / formats
**Actor–Role table** (fill in one row per Actor):

| Actor | Role(s) |
|---|---|
| `[person type]` | `[Initiator / User / Decider / Buyer / Influencer / Gatekeeper]` |

**Five diagnostic questions** to map the Customer Concept for any situation:
1. Who will be the **primary user** of this product?
2. Who will **decide** to purchase it?
3. Who will **pay** for it?
4. Who could **influence** the purchase or use?
5. Who could **prevent** it from being purchased or used?

### (d) Worked examples (from the course)
**Toy Store Shopping (B2C, many roles filled by few Actors):**

| Actor | Role |
|---|---|
| Child | Primary user, Initiator |
| Parent | Decider, Buyer |
| Sibling | Secondary user, Influencer |
| Friend | Secondary user, Influencer |
| Product Reviewer | Influencer |
| Store Salesclerk | Influencer |
| Gov't Regulator | Gatekeeper |

**Complex B2B example (many external Actors):** Engineer = Initiator/User, Engineering Manager = Influencer, Purchasing Manager = Buyer, IT Dept. Manager = Decider/Gatekeeper.

**2-sided market (Google):** Two customer concepts, two products — **Searcher** (user of the Google Search product) and **Advertiser** (user of the Google Ads product). Each side has its own full set of roles.

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- Naming an **organization** ("Government," "Manufacturers") as the customer/Actor instead of a person.
- Collapsing the whole customer to "the buyer" and ignoring Deciders, Influencers, and Gatekeepers who can block or shape the decision.
- Missing the **Gatekeeper** (e.g., a regulator, an IT manager, an EHR admin) — a frequent cause of stalled B2B deals.
- Treating a 2-sided market as one customer concept instead of two, and forgetting each side needs its own product offering.

### (f) Coaching questions
1. "Walk me through everyone involved from first realizing the need to actually using the product — who does each of the five things (initiates, decides, pays, uses, can block it)?"
2. "Is this B2C (one person wears most hats) or B2B (many people, maybe outside the company)? Who signs the check vs. who uses it?"
3. "Is there anyone who could say *no* and kill this — a regulator, an IT admin, a parent, a compliance officer?"
4. "Is this a two-sided market? If so, who are the two customers, and does each side need its own offering?"
5. "Which single Actor will be the **primary User** — the one whose problem we'll center the whole workbook on?"

### (g) Inputs → outputs
- **Consumes:** the rough problem area or product idea.
- **Produces:** an understanding of who's involved and, critically, the **primary User Actor** that the entire Customer Problem Space will focus on. (Course rule: your course project focuses on **1 Actor and 1 JTBD**, and that Actor becomes the primary **User** of the product you later conceive.)

## 1. Fertile Land

### (a) Definition
A **Fertile Land** is an **area of life** — work, school, or play — *that you are familiar with and that interests you.* It is "a source of opportunities where something can begin to develop and grow." You start the whole space from a **blank slate** here: no product idea is assigned; you pick a familiar arena and study the people in it to find problems worth solving.

### (b) Rules & constraints
Rules for identifying a Fertile Land effectively:
- **DO:** provide only a **label** (a short name for the area of life).
- **DON'T** write an explanation of it.
- **DON'T** explain your product idea.
- **DON'T** explain a problem you see.
- **DON'T** limit it to a specific product or platform.
  - **BAD:** "Uber Rides." **GOOD:** "Ride Hailing."
- You **must be familiar** with the Actor and their JTBD inside this land to use it for your project (familiarity is a graded requirement of Product Assignment No. 1).
- **The most promising Fertile Lands are changing a lot** — changes in society, technology, politics, regulation, or environment create innovation opportunities. Prefer arenas in flux.

### (c) Template / format
> **Fertile Land = `[short label of an area of life]`**

That's it — a label, nothing more.

### (d) Worked examples (from the course)
Past-student Fertile Lands (label only): Ride Hailing, Public Transportation, Online gaming, Childcare, Air Travel, Job searching, Grocery shopping, Software development, Home healthcare, Personal finance, Cooking meals, Apartment renting, Fitness workouts, Mentorship. The instructor's own example: **Language Learning**. Exemplar workbooks: "Online grocery shopping," "Podcast creation / podcasting industry," "Dental Healthcare sector," "EV charging / EV road-tripping," "DIY Home Decorating," "Software quality assurance / testing."

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- Writing a **product or brand** name ("Uber Rides") instead of the area of life ("Ride Hailing").
- Writing a **sentence, an explanation, a problem, or a solution** where only a label belongs.
- Choosing an area you **aren't actually familiar with** — you'll lack the depth to find real problems (exemplars that worked leaned hard on lived experience: a heavy user of the arena sizing a problem in a domain they personally live in).
- Picking a **stagnant** arena when a changing one offers more opportunity.

### (f) Coaching questions
1. "What's an area of life — work, school, or play — you genuinely know from the inside?"
2. "Can you say it as a 2–3 word label, without naming a company or a product?"
3. "Is this arena changing a lot right now (tech, regulation, society)? Where's the churn?"
4. "If you already have a product idea, what area of life does it live in? Work backwards to the land."
5. "Are there lots of different kinds of people in this land you could study for problems?"

### (g) Inputs → outputs
- **Consumes:** your familiarity/interests (or a pre-existing product idea, worked backward).
- **Produces:** the labeled arena inside which you'll identify Actors. Feeds directly into **Actor**.

## 2. Actor

### (a) Definition
An **Actor** is *a representative class of people that is useful for innovation.* It puts your focus on the customer. You want to build a product that solves a problem **many alike people** have, so an Actor is a group of alike individuals who **perform similar activities, have similar behaviors, and possess similar characteristics.** There can be **many distinct Actors** in one Fertile Land, each likely to have different problems.

> **Actor vs. Customer Role vs. Persona.** A *Role* is the action taken in buying/using (Initiator, User, etc.). A *Persona* is a written fictitious profile of a representative customer — **you are NOT asked to build a persona in this course.** The Actor is the level of analysis between the two: a named class of people.

### (b) The 3 Rules (must satisfy)
1. **The Actor must represent a class of PEOPLE** that is useful for innovation. It must **NOT** represent an organization or department. — BAD: "Government," "Manufacturers."
2. **The Actor must be a clear and concise LABEL.** Not a sentence or phrase. — BAD: "a teacher in an elementary school." GOOD: "Elementary School Teacher."
3. **Not too general, not too specific.**
   - Too general — BAD: "Customer," "User," "Consumer."
   - Too specific — BAD: "Carnegie Mellon University Teachers," "EV Drivers in Pittsburgh."
   - **Do NOT include geography** in the Actor for now (e.g., "College Teachers in the USA") — geography belongs later in market segmentation.

**Abstraction laddering** (how general/specific to be): the higher the abstraction, the more problems you may find but with less depth; the lower, the more depth but you may miss an attractive problem. Example ladder: "Teacher" (all teachers) → "University Teachers" (all teachers at universities) → "MBA Teachers" (all teachers of MBA students). Each is "right" — it is your choice.

### (c) Template / format
> **Actor = `[concise label for a class of people]`**

### (d) Worked examples (from the course)
| Fertile Land | Actors |
|---|---|
| Sporting games | Player, Coach, Officials, Fan |
| Ride-hailing | Driver, Rider |
| Online shopping | Shopper, Merchant |
| Dentistry | Dentist, Dental Hygienist, Dental Assistant, Dental Patient |
| Universities | Teacher, Teaching Assistant, Student, Professor |

Instructor's choice: Fertile Land = Language Learning → Actor = **Language Learner** (he dropped "New" as redundant). Exemplar Actors: "Podcast creator," "Dental Patient," "EV Driver," "DIY Home Decorator," "Software Tester," "Parents of toddlers."

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- Naming an **organization** ("a hospital," "manufacturers") — Actors are people.
- Writing a **phrase/sentence** instead of a label.
- Being **too generic** ("Customer/User/Consumer") — you can't find specific problems for an undefined class.
- Being **too specific** or including **geography** prematurely.
- Choosing an Actor you're **not familiar with** (you can't credibly infer their outcomes).
- A generic actor ("the gamer of open-world games" — an entire player type) is weaker than a sharply drawn class (exemplar critique).

### (f) Coaching questions
1. "Inside your Fertile Land, list the distinct kinds of people. Which class do you know best?"
2. "Say your Actor as a 2–4 word label — no verbs, no geography, no organization."
3. "Is 'Customer/User' too vague to find a real problem? Can you name the *specific* class?"
4. "Try abstraction laddering: give me a broader and a narrower version. Which level lets you find a worthwhile, deep problem?"
5. "Will this Actor become the **primary user** of the product you eventually build? (It must.)"

### (g) Inputs → outputs
- **Consumes:** the Fertile Land.
- **Produces:** one Actor (a class of people) whose Jobs you'll study. Feeds **JTBD**. This Actor becomes the primary **User** of the later product.

## 3. Job To Be Done (JTBD)

### (a) Definition
A **JTBD** is *the fundamental task the Actor wants to complete* — something the Actor **does** (an activity). You study the Job because it reveals the problems people have getting it done. "People hire products and services to help them get that Job done better." (Classic framing: the JTBD isn't "buy a drill," it's **"make a hole."**) A JTBD orientation keeps you **customer-centric, not product-centric.** An Actor can have **many** JTBDs.

### (b) The 4 Rules (must satisfy)
1. **Functional.** It must be a **task** the Actor is trying to complete. — BAD: "To feel confident" (not a task).
2. **Singular.** Only **one** task per JTBD statement. — BAD: "Write and test software code" (two jobs). GOOD: two separate JTBDs — "Write software code," "Test software code."
3. **Accurate.** No other concepts baked in — no actor, outcome, benefit, etc. — BAD: "Get a full-time job **faster**" ("faster" is a benefit). GOOD: "Get a full-time job."
4. **Appropriate.** It is an actual Job the Actor is really trying to get done.

**Reframing / abstraction laddering** applies to JTBDs too. JTBD, Stages, and Tasks are the same kind of thing at different altitudes. Make a *stage* into a JTBD to focus deeper; make a JTBD into a *stage* of a higher JTBD to think broader. Example ladder (Actor = homeowner): "to decorate a home" → "to hang a picture" → "to make a hole." **Reframing the JTBD changes the outcomes, the problem, and the solution.**

**Concept grouping tip:** if you brainstorm many low-level JTBDs, group them under a higher-level JTBD (e.g., a Professor's "Give lecture / Coach students / Assess skills / Give feedback / Track progress" group up into "Develop talent" and "Teach courses").

### (c) Templates / format
> **JTBD = To `[verb]` `[object]`** (a single functional task, no benefits/actors/outcomes)

Examples of the form: "To learn a new language," "Get a full-time job," "Restore blood flow," "Move goods into storage," "Get to a destination."

### (d) Worked examples (from the course)
| Actor | JTBD |
|---|---|
| Student job seeker | Get a full-time job |
| Music enthusiast | Listen to music |
| Interventional Cardiologist | Restore blood flow |
| Farmer | Grow a crop |
| Language Learner | To learn a new language |

Exemplar JTBDs: "To buy groceries from online store," "To create a podcast episode," "To get a cavity filling procedure," "To charge the car," "To decorate a living space."

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- Writing a **feeling/state** instead of a task ("to feel confident," "to be productive").
- Cramming **two jobs** into one ("write and test code").
- Baking in a **benefit/outcome** ("get a job *faster*") — that belongs in Outcomes.
- Including the **Actor** or a **use case** in the JTBD sentence.
- **Altitude mismatch:** stating a broad job ("develop an application") but actually serving a narrow sub-step ("deploy quickly") — pick the right level, or note the bridge explicitly (exemplar critique).

### (f) Coaching questions
1. "Finish this: *To ___* — one verb, one task. Does it pass 'functional, singular, accurate, appropriate'?"
2. "Did any benefit or feeling sneak in (faster, easier, confident)? Strip it out — it's an outcome, not the job."
3. "Is this actually two jobs? If so, split them and pick one."
4. "Try laddering up and down: what's the bigger job this is a step of, and what's a smaller step inside it? Which altitude reveals the best problem?"
5. "Is this a job your Actor genuinely does, and one you know how it's done today?"

### (g) Inputs → outputs
- **Consumes:** the Actor.
- **Produces:** one JTBD that frames the whole problem space. Feeds **Outcomes** (results/experiences at Job completion) and the **Customer Journey Map** (the steps of doing this Job). Course project focuses on **1 JTBD**.

## 4. Outcomes (Actual & Desired) — the most important concept

### (a) Definition
**Outcomes** are *the results and experiences the Actor gets when the JTBD is completed.* (Other terms: goals, results, end results, consequences, impact.) Outcomes come in two states:
- **Actual Outcome** = the outcome the Actor gets **now** (current state, **without** your product).
- **Desired Outcome** = the **better** outcome the Actor wants (future state, **with** your product).

**Principle:** customers are most likely to acquire and use products that help them get **better** outcomes. Outcomes are declared the **most important concept to master** because they are the basis for: defining problems, determining customer value, measuring success, and making smarter trade-offs.

An outcome idea in "raw" form is **not good enough**. A **well-defined outcome** has a **metric** and a **value**, so the team knows the target, how much better is needed, and whether the effort was worth it.

### (b) Rules, constraints & the metric taxonomy
- Product Assignment No. 1 needs **≥ 1** raw outcome; Assignment No. 2 needs **≥ 3 well-defined** outcomes (measurable, with metrics + values). There are often more than 3 important outcomes.
- Use **"Average" (or "Median")** with metrics, because you're building for many customers.
- **Measure outcomes that occur at the END of the JTBD** — not long before, not long after. — Actor: Graduate Student, JTBD: Apply for a Job → BAD metric: "Ave. No. Job Offers" (occurs long after). GOOD: "Ave. No. Applications Submitted."
- A **metric must be concisely named — a label, not a sentence.** "A metric needs to fit on a dashboard."
- **Value** answers "how much." Value may be **quantitative or qualitative** (e.g., "Low/High" or a %).
- Every metric needs a **Measurement Method** (calculation logic, units, assumptions, data sources) for consistent counting and reliable comparison.
- **Sourcing (for this course):** use your own experience/intuition, or credible published sources (Statista, Data.gov, NCHS, trade associations), or GenAI — but **"don't trust, verify, cite the source."** These suffice for *learning*, not for building a real successful product.
- For **Desired Outcomes**, set a goal *significantly better* than the actual — consider "what have the best achieved?" and the Actor's own goal.

**Three kinds of metrics (how to quantify different outcomes):**

| Metric type | When it applies | How to quantify |
|---|---|---|
| **Obvious** | You can directly observe & count the outcome (Effort, Duration, Speed, Cost, Productivity, Errors, Defects, Support Calls) | Count/observe directly — no subjective interpretation needed. |
| **Behavioral** | You can't directly observe the outcome (Higher Quality, Higher Skill, Improved Usability, Reduced Cognitive Load, Greater Insight, Higher Trust, Convenience) | Use a **behavioral indicator** — a countable action/decision that *predicts* the outcome. E.g., code-review quality → "Ave. Quality Checklist Completion Rate." |
| **Sentiment** | The outcome is a feeling (Greater Happiness, More Fun, Motivation, Reduced Risk, Comfort, Confidence, Enjoyment) | **Infer** it from self-reported perceptions/opinions/feelings via a sentiment survey/assessment. E.g., "Ave. Enjoyment Rating is 65%" or "45% rate Happiness as Low." |

You may also **invent** a metric (BMI and IQ were invented to quantify weight-for-height and intelligence).

### (c) The 5-Step Method + templates
**5 Steps to Create Well-Defined Outcomes:**
1. **Conceive** several outcome *ideas* for the Actor + JTBD.
2. **Develop a Metric** (quantifiable indicator) for each idea.
3. **Identify Values** for the Actual and Desired outcomes.
4. **Create Outcome Statements** for each.
5. **Determine a Measurement Method** for each metric.

**Step 1 idea-generation prompts:** When the JTBD is completed — what results/experiences does the Actor want? What is the impact? What goals? What do they want to achieve? What does success look like?

**Step 2 metric-generation prompts:** What could be counted? What do others count? What could be calculated? What tracks progress? If unique, can we invent a metric?

**Step 4 Outcome-Statement templates:**
> **Actual Outcome:** The `[metric]` is `[value]`. — *e.g., "The Ave. Salary Increase is 3%."*
> **Desired Outcome:** The `[metric]` will be `[value]`. — *e.g., "The Ave. Salary Increase will be 20%."*

Always write them as **pairs** (1 Actual + 1 Desired), same metric, only the value differs. Sometimes include units (e.g., "per year").

**Step 5 Measurement-Method** captures: calculation logic + units + assumptions + data sources.

### (d) Worked examples (from the course)
**Language Learning (Actor = Language Learner, JTBD = to learn a new language):**

| Metric | Actual Outcome | Desired Outcome | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ave. No. Words Learned | 2–3 | 2,000 (1st year) | ~1,997 words |
| Ave. CEFR Proficiency Level | A1 | A2 | 1 proficiency level |
| Ave. Total Study Hours | 0 hrs/yr | 400 hrs/yr | 400 hours/yr |

*Values sourced from UNESCO, Council of Europe (CEFR), and the instructor's own experience.*

**Measurement Method example** — Metric: *Ave. Body Mass Index* → "BMI = weight ÷ height². Units: weight in kilograms, height in meters." Metric: *Ave. Salary Increase (%)* → "Take the difference between post-graduation starting salary and prior salary, divide by prior salary, multiply by 100."

**Sentiment example** — Outcome idea "More Enjoyment" → Metric "Ave. Enjoyment Rating" → Actual "The Ave. Enjoyment Rating is 65%" (or "Low").

Exemplar patterns (from strong workbooks): one strong workbook — Ave. Creation Steps 8 → 4; Ave. Software Tools 3 → 1; Ave. File I/O Time 4 min → 0. Another strong workbook — Ave. Job Search Time 20 hrs/week → 2 hrs/week. These reuse **one metric** across Actual and Desired, differing only in value.

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- Leaving outcomes as **vague ideas** ("little time," "a lot of time") — team and customers interpret them differently. Not good enough.
- A metric with a **value but no measurement method** (no boundaries/units/aggregation) — can't be measured consistently.
- Measuring an outcome that happens **long after the JTBD** ("No. Job Offers" for an "apply for a job" JTBD).
- Writing metrics as **sentences** instead of dashboard-ready labels.
- Capturing **only Desired** (or only Actual) — you need both to reveal the gap.
- Restricting to **only quantitative** results and ignoring qualitative experiences (satisfaction, disappointment) — both count.
- Failing to specify the **unit of analysis** (per instance? per campaign? per year?).
- Setting a Desired Outcome that's barely above Actual — the gap is too thin to be a "worthwhile problem" (exemplar critique: desired only 5–8 points above actual).

### (f) Coaching questions
1. "When the Job is *done*, what results and experiences does your Actor actually get? List several ideas."
2. "For each idea, what could you count or calculate? Is it obvious, behavioral (needs a predictor), or a sentiment (needs a survey)?"
3. "Give me the Actual value (today) and a Desired value (significantly better) for the *same* metric — where's the number from, and did you cite it?"
4. "Does this outcome happen exactly when the Job finishes — not a long time before or after?"
5. "Write the pair as statements: 'The [metric] is [X]' / 'The [metric] will be [Y].' Would a teammate read the metric the same way you do?"
6. "How exactly would you measure this — start boundary, end boundary, units, average across whom?"

### (g) Inputs → outputs
- **Consumes:** Actor + JTBD.
- **Produces:** ≥3 well-defined **paired** Outcome Statements (metric + value + measurement method). The **gap** between each Actual/Desired pair defines a candidate **Problem**; the metrics/values feed **Problem Sizing**; and the outcomes seed the **Problem Category** and **Problem Statement**. Outcomes are also the basis for the later Customer Value Space.

## 5. Problem = Gap + Causes

### (a) Definition
A **Problem** is comprised of the **gap** between the Actual and Desired outcomes **and the cause(s) of that gap.** The **gap is the effect**; the **causes are the reasons the gap occurs.** ("Cause and Effect" = "the direct relationship between an action or event and its consequences or results.") You conceive and build products to **address the causes** of the gap to solve the problem.

- **Gap** = Desired Outcome value − Actual Outcome value (per metric). *"The bigger the gap, the bigger the problem."* If there's **no gap**, the customer is already succeeding — there's **no problem to solve.**
- A **cause** answers: *"Why doesn't the Actor get a better outcome?"*

### (b) Rules, cause-finding methods & the 4 cause types
**Gap:** every Actual/Desired outcome pair produces (at most) one gap. You'll typically have multiple gaps (~1 per outcome pair) and will later **select** one problem to solve.

**Methods to identify causes** (qualitative → statistical): Human Judgment, **5 Whys**, Task Analysis, Customer Journey Analysis, **Ishikawa Fishbone (Cause-and-Effect) diagram**, Value Stream Analysis, Business Process Analysis, Fault Tree Analysis, FMEA, Regression, Pareto Analysis, and many more. When interacting with real customers, **you MUST "drill-down"** to the causes.

**The 4 types of causes:**

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Root cause** | The underlying/fundamental/initial reason the problem exists. Eliminating it **eliminates the problem** and improves outcomes. |
| **Contributing cause** | Helps create the problem but isn't sufficient alone; can make it more severe, larger, more frequent, or more likely. Addressing it improves outcomes. |
| **Deterministic cause** | Reliably and predictably leads to the problem given the same circumstances — certain to occur. *(e.g., a software bug: find it, fix it, gone.)* |
| **Probabilistic cause** | Increases the *likelihood* of the problem but isn't certain; often uncontrollable factors. *(e.g., wear-and-tear of a mechanical system.)* |

**5 Whys Technique:** Reflect on what's causing the gap, then **question the answer, repeat ~4 more times** to reach the root cause. (Root cause "doesn't always take 5 whys.")

**Fishbone / Ishikawa (from the readings):** a 4-step root-cause method — (1) **Identify the problem** (write who/what/when/where; box it as the "fish head" with a horizontal "spine"); (2) **Work out major factors** (draw a "bone" per factor; seed factors with a model like McKinsey **7S** — Strategy/Structure/Systems/Shared values/Skills/Style/Staff — or the **4Ps** — Product/Place/Price/Promotion); (3) **Identify possible causes** off each bone, breaking complex causes into sub-causes; (4) **Analyze the diagram** — the causes are **hypotheses**; investigate/test the most likely ones. Sharpen the problem statement first with **CATWOE** (Customers, Actors, Transformation, World view, Owner, Environment). Team variant: **CEDAC** (one cause per sticky note, cluster onto bones).

### (c) Templates / formats
**5 Whys template:**
> **Gap:** `[the outcome gap]`
> Why? `[cause 1]` → Why? `[cause 2]` → Why? `[cause 3]` → Why? `[cause 4]` → Why? `[root cause]`

**Fishbone skeleton:** `[PROBLEM = fish head]` — spine — `[FACTOR 1]`, `[FACTOR 2]`, … each with `[possible cause]` and `[sub-cause]` lines.

**Problem = Gap + Causes** (record both halves):
> **Gap:** `[metric] gap of [amount]` · **Cause(s):** `[root cause] + [contributing causes]`

### (d) Worked examples (from the course)
**5 Whys (course example):**
> Gap: Ave. Course Pass Rate was 50% lower than desired.
> Why? Low scores on exams. → Why? Didn't understand key concepts. → Why? Didn't complete practice exercises. → Why? Didn't have enough time. → Why? **Bad time management** *(root cause).*

**Fishbone (reading example):** "Uncooperative branch office" as the fish head; factors Site / Task / People / Equipment / Control as bones. The payoff: instead of assuming people were "being difficult," the diagram pointed to a structural fix — brief the Branch Manager on the new strategy.

Exemplar patterns: one strong workbook's root cause = "too many different applications needed to create a podcast → unnecessary steps." Another workbook's 5 Whys terminated at "limited resources, financial constraints, lack of supportive community."

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- Jumping to a **solution before mapping causes** — you fix only part of the problem and it recurs.
- Listing only the **obvious** cause and stopping (the whole point of Fishbone/5 Whys is to go past the obvious).
- Making a **subjective snap judgment** ("they're just being difficult") instead of a structural diagnosis.
- **Ignoring the causes entirely** — most learners remember the gap but forget "Problem = Gap + *Causes*." You can't remove a cause you never named.
- Treating fishbone possible-causes as **proven** rather than **hypotheses** to test.
- Asserting a root cause without validation, then proposing a solution that never addresses *why incumbents haven't already solved it* (exemplar critique).

### (f) Coaching questions
1. "Which outcome gap are you focusing on, and how big is it (Desired − Actual)?"
2. "Ask 'why' five times starting from that gap — where do you bottom out? Is that a root cause or still a symptom?"
3. "Is your cause a *root* cause (fix it and the problem's gone) or a *contributing* one (makes it worse)? Deterministic or probabilistic?"
4. "Seed a fishbone: what are the major factors (people, process, systems, tools, environment)? Any hidden causes you're missing?"
5. "Are these causes proven, or are they hypotheses you'd need to test with customers?"
6. "If you built something that removed this cause, would the gap actually close?"

### (g) Inputs → outputs
- **Consumes:** the Actual/Desired Outcome pairs (for the gap) and the Customer Journey Map (which helps pinpoint *where* causes occur).
- **Produces:** a defined **Problem** = the selected gap + its root/contributing causes. Feeds the **Causes layer** of the Journey Map, the **Problem Statement** (which needs a key cause), and later the Solution Space (products address the causes).

## 6. Customer Journey Map (7 layers)

### (a) Definition
A **Customer Journey** is *the series of steps and interactions the Actor takes to do their JTBD.* A **Customer Journey Map** is a **VISUAL** depiction of that journey. It models the **current-state** journey of the *typical/average* Actor, covers the **entire** Job (start to finish, no missing stages), and gives the big-picture **context** of the problem space. It deepens understanding and, crucially, **helps identify the causes of the gap** between actual and desired outcomes. (Related concepts: Task/Workflow/Business-Process/Value-Stream Analysis, User Journey, Customer Experience Journey.)

Structure: journeys have **Stages** (the main steps, in sequence) each broken into **Tasks** (the actions within a stage). Stages and Tasks are the same kind of thing as a JTBD — different altitudes (reframing).

### (b) The 7 Layers + rules
**The 7 layers of a Customer Journey Map:**

| # | Layer | What it captures |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Context** | The Actor, their JTBD, and the applicable Use Cases (plus the Outcomes). |
| 2 | **Stages** | The main steps to complete the JTBD, in sequence. |
| 3 | **Tasks** | The actions taken during a stage. |
| 4 | **Experiences** | Strong feelings/perceptions the Actor has during the journey (emotions). |
| 5 | **Information** | Key information/data used or produced during a stage (a stage may have none). |
| 6 | **Touchpoints** | Points of interaction/integration/exchange between people and/or objects (a stage may have none). |
| 7 | **Causes** | A cause of the gap between an actual and desired outcome, captured in the stage where it occurs (a stage may or may not have one). |

**Rules & numeric guidance:**
- The map **must be a visualization** (Assignment 3) — not a list.
- It covers the **entire JTBD** and ends **when the JTBD is completed** — **no stages/tasks that occur AFTER** the Job is done. (BAD: "Attend Company Orientation" appears in a "get a full-time job" journey — that's after the job is gotten.) Map and JTBD must **agree**.
- **You decide where the journey starts** — identify a **trigger**: a common decision, event, or action that starts the Job. (e.g., "Career Advisor requests a resume.")
- **How many Stages? Aim for 5–6** (4 or 7 sometimes fine). <4 → too many tasks per stage; >7 → too few tasks per stage.
- **How many Tasks? 4–7 per stage** is usually effective. >7 → revise the stages; <4 → move/rename.
- **Write Stages & Tasks as "Verb – Adjective – Noun"** — action-oriented, concise, fits on a map.
- **Experiences layer:** negative experiences = signals of opportunity; positive experiences = keep them (a source of differentiation, value, loyalty).
- **Touchpoints:** issues/breakdowns/bad experiences often occur here — attractive opportunities.
- **Causes:** you may pinpoint the specific item causing the gap with a number/symbol.
- Tools: Figma (course template), Miro, Mural, Illustrator, Excel, paper, Smaply, AI tools. Row order isn't very important.

### (c) Template / format (visual, layered)
```
CONTEXT:  Actor | JTBD | Use Cases | Outcomes
          ┌─────────┬─────────┬─────────┬─────────┬─────────┐
STAGES →  │ Stage 1 │ Stage 2 │ Stage 3 │  ...    │ Stage N │
TASKS →   │ • task  │ • task  │ • task  │         │ • task  │
EXPER. →  │ 😊/😟   │         │  😠     │         │         │
INFO →    │ [data]  │         │ [data]  │         │         │
TOUCH. →  │ [point] │ [point] │         │         │         │
CAUSES →  │         │  ① cause│         │         │ ② cause │
```
Stage/Task naming slot: **`[Verb] [Adjective] [Noun]`** (e.g., "Set Job Goals," "Prepare Resume(s)").

### (d) Worked examples (from the course)
**"Get a Full-Time Job" (Actor = Unemployed Job Seeker):** 7 stages — 1. Set Job Goals · 2. Prepare Resume(s) · 3. Plan Job Search · 4. Conduct Job Search · 5. Conduct Job Interviews · 6. Evaluate Job Offers · 7. Accept Job Offer. Outcomes anchored on the map: Ave. Time to Employment = 5 months; Ave. Job Acquisition Effort = 30 hrs/week; Ave. Job Compensation = $65,470. Sample layers: *Tasks* (Stage 4) — contact recruiters, search job sites, set up alerts, complete applications, attach resumes, write cover letters; *Experiences* — "apprehensive"; *Information* — job postings, resume, Glassdoor reviews, recruiter contact; *Touchpoints* — smartphone, laptop, web browser, resume upload, email app; *Causes* — "significant effort to conduct job searches and complete each Job Application."

Course-flagged errors in student maps: a "find a vending machine" JTBD whose map showed stages *after* finding the machine (map and JTBD disagree — fix one or both); several maps missing the Causes layer.

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- Submitting a **list instead of a visualization.**
- **Stages after the JTBD is done** / map disagrees with the JTBD.
- **Omitting the Causes layer** (the layer that most directly feeds problem-solving).
- Too many/too few stages or tasks (violating 5–6 stages, 4–7 tasks).
- Writing stages/tasks as **sentences** instead of Verb-Adjective-Noun labels.
- No defined **trigger** — the start of the journey is arbitrary.
- Referencing the map's key artifacts (context/deployment diagrams) but leaving them empty (exemplar critique: load-bearing diagrams present only as captions).

### (f) Coaching questions
1. "What event triggers the start of this Job — the moment the Actor begins?"
2. "What are the 5–6 main stages, in order, from trigger to 'Job done'? Any stage sneaking in *after* completion?"
3. "Break each stage into 4–7 tasks. Any stage bulging past 7 or shrinking below 4?"
4. "Where along the journey does the Actor feel something strong (frustrated, anxious, delighted)? Those are your opportunities."
5. "At which stage do the outcome gaps actually get caused? Mark the cause there."
6. "What info/data and what touchpoints appear at each stage — any missing, stale, or broken ones?"

### (g) Inputs → outputs
- **Consumes:** Actor, JTBD, Use Cases, Outcomes (Context layer), and the Causes analysis.
- **Produces:** a visual model that (i) provides problem-space **context**, (ii) **locates causes** by stage, and (iii) surfaces opportunities at negative experiences and touchpoints. Feeds the **Problem** (causes), **Use Cases** (context), and later the Solution Space (user flows).

## 7. Use Cases (4 naming rules)

### (a) Definition
A **Use Case** is *a specific circumstance or set of circumstances in which the Actor does the JTBD* — a **scenario** in which the Job is done. Useful question: *"What are some distinct circumstances or scenarios in which the [Job] is done?"* There are **frequently multiple** use cases, and **each may lead to different Outcomes** — and therefore different problems, causes, and customer journeys. Use Cases provide **context** and shape which product, pricing, features, and marketing you build.

Alternative business terms: **Usage Occasion** (CPG), **Scenario / Operational Scenario** (software engineering), **Application** (manufacturing), **Indication** (pharma).

### (b) The 4 Naming Rules (must satisfy)
1. **Clearly named (labelled).** Specific and unambiguous.
2. **Accurate.** It identifies the use case *only* — it does NOT include an Actor, JTBD, problem, outcome, etc.
3. **Important.** Significant for *many* people your Actor represents — common, noteworthy.
4. **Distinct.** Distinctively different from each other use case.

**Selecting use cases:** your product will be conceived, designed, built, and marketed for the use cases you **select to serve** — choose important ones (significant, common for many Actors).

### (c) Template / format
> **Use Case = `[concise scenario label]`** (no actor, no job, no outcome inside it)

### (d) Worked examples (from the course)
**Actor: College Professor · JTBD: To teach a course** →
- **Effective** use cases: Traditional Classroom courses · Synchronous Online courses · Asynchronous Online courses · Hybrid courses · Laboratory courses.
- **Ineffective** (with the reason): "Teach a Traditional Classroom course" (that's a JTBD/task) · "Better Synchronous Online courses" ("better" is an outcome) · "Courses that can be watched on demand" (that's a description, not a name) · "When teachers are teaching Hybrid courses" (contains an actor + JTBD) · "Classroom 265 Courses" (not important to most Actors).

**Actor: Moviegoer · JTBD: Watch a movie** → Movie Theater · Drive-In Theater · Film Festival · Home Theater System · On Devices · VR headsets · Outdoor Events · Airplane Travel. (The Moviegoer likely has different Outcomes per use case.)

**Actor: Restaurant Patron · JTBD: To have a meal** → Casual Sit-Down · Fine Dining · Business Meeting Dining · Outdoor Dining · Themed Dining · Celebratory Events.

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- Embedding an **Actor, JTBD, outcome, or problem** in the use-case name ("When teachers teach hybrid courses," "Better online courses").
- Writing a **description or a task** instead of a **name**.
- Listing use cases that **aren't important** to most Actors (too niche — "Classroom 265 Courses").
- Use cases that **overlap** rather than being distinct.
- Introducing use cases early and then **never carrying them through** to segments/solution/pricing (exemplar critique).

### (f) Coaching questions
1. "In what distinct scenarios does your Actor do this Job? Name 3–6."
2. "Is each name a clean *label* — no actor, no verb, no 'better/faster' outcome inside it?"
3. "Is each one important to *many* of your Actors, or too niche?"
4. "Are they truly distinct, or do some overlap?"
5. "Which use case(s) will you *select to serve*, and would the Outcomes differ across them?"

### (g) Inputs → outputs
- **Consumes:** Actor + JTBD (and informs/records in the Journey Map's Context layer).
- **Produces:** a set of named scenarios that becomes part of the **Context** and shapes which problems/outcomes you pursue; feeds Market segmentation and Solution design. The selected use case(s) scope the whole downstream product.

## 8. Problem Sizing (5 methods)

### (a) Definition
**Problem size** is *a measure of a problem's impact* — how big an issue it is, how much "pain." Sizing helps you: evaluate if a problem is **worthwhile** to solve, **prioritize** among problems, conceive/design/validate a solution, **set a price**, and **communicate** why the solution is needed.

### (b) The 5 Methods + rules
| # | Method | Definition / formula |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Per instance** | The size (amount) of the gap **each time** the average Actor does the JTBD. |
| 2 | **Annualized per actor** | Sum total of the gaps for the average Actor per year = **per-instance gap × frequency of JTBD per year.** |
| 3 | **Total impact, annually** | Sum total of the gap for **all** Actors per year = **per-instance gap × frequency × number of Actors.** |
| 4 | **Monetary Impact** | The above sizes expressed in money per year (a.k.a. the **"cost of inaction"**). |
| 5 | **Rate of Change** | The trend/rate per year at which the problem size (gap, frequency, probability, # actors) is changing. |

**Rules & guidance:**
- **Apply the first 3** methods for the product assignment.
- Sometimes **Annualized-per-Actor = Per-instance** (when the metric is already annualized, e.g., "study hours per year").
- **Number of Actors:** find a **credible, published source** and **cite** it; it's an **estimate** (needn't be exact); do math/assumptions as needed. Try for the **worldwide** number; if unavailable, use a country/region. Adjust the number to match your Actor as named. Sources: Statista, search/GenAI (verify), LinkedIn (professionals — beware current vs. past), professional associations, BLS, Census.
- **Special situations:**
  - *Gap not quantified* → measure only frequency and/or # of affected Actors. ("This problem affects 19.5M college students 12 times per year.")
  - *Frequency is constant/continuous* (all year long) → measure only the gap and/or # of affected Actors. ("1.5B new language learners fall 1 proficiency level short each year.")
  - *Gap is a probability of occurrence* → use a probability and/or # of Actors who may have it. ("255M car drivers have a 5.9% (1 in 17) chance of a car accident per year.")
- **Selecting a problem to solve** (among multiple gaps): consider **size and frequency** (bigger/more is better), **feasibility** (more feasible is better), **viability** (profitable/competitive?), and **strategic fit** with your company.

### (c) Templates / formulas
> **Method 1 (Per instance):** Gap = `[Desired value]` − `[Actual value]` per JTBD instance.
> **Method 2 (Annualized per actor):** `[per-instance gap]` × `[JTBD frequency/year]`.
> **Method 3 (Total impact):** `[per-instance gap]` × `[frequency/year]` × `[# Actors]`.
> **Method 4 (Monetary):** `[problem size]` × `[$ per unit, e.g., wage/hour]`. For an annual total across all Actors, `[problem size]` is the Method 3 total impact (or the Method 2 per-actor size × `[# Actors]`); the Method 2 size alone gives $ per Actor per year. Convert units first (minutes → hours for an hourly wage).
> **Method 5 (Rate of change):** `[% change/year]` applied to any size above.

**Estimation-logic template** (when the number doesn't match your Actor): state related data + assumptions + logic + cited source.

### (d) Worked examples (from the course)
**Airport security (JTBD = get through security checkpoint):**
- *Per instance:* Actual 20–25 min, Desired 5 min → **Gap = 15–20 min/check.**
- *Annualized per actor:* 15–20 min × **3.6 flights/yr** = **54–72 min/passenger/yr.**
- *Total impact:* 15–20 min × 3.6 × **151M passengers** = **8.2B–10.9B minutes/yr** (÷ 60 = **≈136M–181M hours/yr** across all passengers).
- *Monetary:* 54–72 min/passenger/yr = 0.9–1.2 hrs/passenger/yr × $30/hr average wage = $27–$36/passenger/yr; × **151M passengers** = **≈$4.1B–$5.4B/yr** cost of inaction (same as 136M–181M hours × $30/hr).
- *Rate of change:* +12%/yr passengers → time-in-line rising each year.

**Language learning (constant frequency):** Ave. 400 study hours/yr per learner × **1.5B new learners globally** = **600B total study hours/yr.**

**Estimation logic (Actor = Performing Arts College Students, USA):** 150,000 degrees conferred in 2022 × 4 years = 600,000; add grad students (~17% of college students) → 600,000 ÷ 0.83 ≈ **723,000** (Source: Statista).

Exemplar patterns: one strong workbook — 4 extra steps × 40 episodes/yr = 160 extra steps/user/yr, × ~4M creators. Another workbook — per-shopper 1,430 hrs waited/yr, scaled to 60M shoppers × 52 × 27.5 hrs = 85.8B hrs/yr. A third workbook — 91.4M uninsured × 30% × $500 = $13.7B/yr, stated as an explicit auditable formula (each factor sourced or labeled).

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- **Guessing the # of Actors** with no cited source (exemplar critique: "assuming from personal experience" undermines precise-looking numbers).
- **Unit-labeling errors** carried through the math (e.g., "52 days" that should be "52 weeks/orders") — an exemplar mistake.
- **Implausible headline numbers** that don't reconcile (e.g., "20.5 billion hours" that's ~300× off) — destroys credibility.
- Sizing **only one way** (per-instance) and skipping annualized-per-actor and total-impact.
- Mismatching the # of Actors to the Actor **as named** (not adjusting up/down).
- Confusing a **revenue/adoption projection** with problem size, or a projection with TAM.
- Not handling the **special situations** (unquantified gap, constant frequency, probability).

### (f) Coaching questions
1. "What's the gap *each time* the Actor does the Job? Now × how many times per year? Now × how many Actors?"
2. "Where's your Actor count from — is it cited, and does it match your Actor as named (worldwide if possible)?"
3. "Can you convert the annual size to dollars (cost of inaction) using a wage or price?"
4. "Is the frequency continuous, the gap unquantified, or is this a probability? Use the matching special-case method."
5. "Do the units line up all the way through — minutes vs. hours, weeks vs. days, per-instance vs. per-year?"
6. "Among your gaps, which is biggest/most frequent, feasible, and viable enough to be the one you solve?"

### (g) Inputs → outputs
- **Consumes:** the Outcome gaps + JTBD frequency + # of Actors.
- **Produces:** 1–3 quantified problem sizes (per-instance, annualized-per-actor, total-impact; optionally monetary + rate-of-change) that justify the problem is worthwhile, feed the **Problem Statement** (needs 1–2 sizes), inform pricing, and set up Market Space (TAM).

## 9. Problem Category

### (a) Definition
A **Problem Category** is *a short label that identifies a type (class) of problems.* It's a concise answer to *"What problem are you solving?"* — used to **introduce** a problem before explaining it. Categories help communication and retrieval (our brains categorize; a good category helps your audience understand quickly).

### (b) Rules & constraints
- **Keep it short — 2 to 3 words, maybe 4.**
- Either **invent it** (you decide the words — your **outcome statements** are a great starting point; if it resonates with customers, keep it; if not, change it) **or** find/use the **language already used in your problem space** (websites in the space, or ask GenAI for a short label of the problem you're solving).

### (c) Template / format
> **Problem Category = `[2–4 word "[Thing] problem" label]`**

### (d) Worked examples (from the course)
| Underlying problem | Problem Category |
|---|---|
| Long time-in-line at airport security | **Airport Security Checkpoint problem** |
| Time to prepare meals | **Meal Preparation problem** |
| High stress while commuting | **Commuter Stress problem** |
| Difficulty finding freelancers | **Freelancer Discovery problem** |

Exemplar categories: "Podcaster experience problem," "High-cost dental services," "Childhood Development Discrepancy," "Application deployment problem," "Web-application defect leakage."

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- A **long phrase or sentence** instead of a 2–4 word label.
- A label that describes the **solution** rather than the problem class.
- A vague label that doesn't map to the outcome gaps you defined.

### (f) Coaching questions
1. "In 2–4 words, what *class* of problem is this — '[something] problem'?"
2. "Does your category come straight out of your outcome statements?"
3. "Is this the language your customers/problem-space actually use? Would it resonate with them?"
4. "Are you accidentally naming the solution instead of the problem?"

### (g) Inputs → outputs
- **Consumes:** the Outcomes and the defined Problem.
- **Produces:** a short label used to **introduce** the problem (in the Problem Statement, pitches, and the Market/Value spaces).

## 10. Problem Statement (4 templates, solution-free)

### (a) Definition
A **Problem Statement** is *a concise description of the problem that needs to be addressed.* It's used for **communication** — especially to people outside the product team — and helps the team focus. It is the culminating artifact of the Customer Problem Space (the "Brief" level, for communication).

### (b) The 4 Elements + rules
An effective Problem Statement includes exactly these **4 elements**:
1. **The Actor**
2. **The outcome gap**
3. **A key cause of the gap**
4. **The problem size** (1 or 2 sizes)

**Hard rule:** it is **solution-free** — it does **NOT** discuss a product idea. (You don't have to use the templates; you may modify them to be clear and concise. No colored fonts needed in the assignment.)

### (c) The 4 Templates (fill-in slots)
**Template 1 — Who-What-Why:**
> `[Actor]` experience an average of `[outcome gap]`, because `[key cause]`. This leads to `[problem size]`. Addressing this problem will `[desired outcome]`.

**Template 2 — Impact-Driven:**
> Due to `[cause]`, `[actual outcome]` occurs, leading to `[problem size]`. Solving this problem will `[desired outcome]`.

**Template 3 — Gap:**
> Currently, `[current state]`, which leads to `[gap(s)]`. This results in `[problem size + impact]`. A solution is needed to `[desired state]`.

**Template 4 — (Actor-want):**
> `[Actor]` want `[desired outcome]`, but currently face `[gap]`, resulting in `[problem size]`. This is primarily due to `[key cause(s)]`. Addressing these issues would `[impact]`.

### (d) Worked examples (from the course)
**Template 1 (airport security):** "Airline passengers experience an average of **15–20 minutes more Time-In-Line per trip** than the best airports, because **current screening processes are slow and inefficient**. This leads to a cumulative **Time-In-Line of 54 to 72 minutes per passenger per year**. Addressing this problem will **reduce Time-In-Line to 5 minutes per trip**."

**Template 4 (a strong exemplar, podcasting):** "**Podcasters** want to **create new episodes in 2 steps** but currently take an average of **8 steps per episode**, resulting in **160 extra steps per year for the average of 40 episodes** they create. This is primarily due to **too many applications to navigate when creating a podcast**. Addressing this issue would eliminate over **640M unnecessary steps each year collectively of all podcasters**."

*(Note how each contains all 4 elements — Actor, gap, key cause, size(s) — and names no product.)*

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- **Slipping in a solution** ("an app that…") — the statement must be solution-free.
- **Missing one of the 4 elements** (most often the *key cause* or a *size*).
- Using **vague, unquantified** gaps/sizes instead of the numbers from Outcomes and Problem Sizing.
- Over-writing into **marketing hype** ("beacon of change," "paradigm shift") that buries the crisp elements (exemplar critique).
- The statement's numbers **contradicting** the Outcomes/Sizing sections upstream.

### (f) Coaching questions
1. "Give me one paragraph with all four: the Actor, the outcome gap (with numbers), a key cause, and 1–2 problem sizes."
2. "Did any hint of a product/solution sneak in? Strip it — this is solution-free."
3. "Do the numbers here match your Outcomes and Problem Sizing exactly?"
4. "Which template fits best — Who-What-Why, Impact-Driven, Gap, or Actor-want?"
5. "Would someone outside your team understand the problem — and its size — from this alone?"

### (g) Inputs → outputs
- **Consumes:** Actor, the outcome gap, a key cause, and the problem size(s) — i.e., it rolls up the whole Customer Problem Space.
- **Produces:** the concise, solution-free statement that closes this space and hands off to the **Market Space** (and later the Product Narrative). It is the communication artifact stakeholders read first.

## Strong vs. Weak — grounded in the exemplar workbooks

**What "strong" looks like (from the top workbooks):**
- **One golden thread of numbers.** The best workbooks pick 2–3 core metrics early — a friction count, a frequency, a wage/value rate — and reuse them *verbatim* across Outcomes → Problem → Sizing → Statement so everything reconciles as one argument.
- **Outcome-gap rigor.** Actor → JTBD → Use Case → Actual Outcome → Desired Outcome → Problem (numeric gap) → Cause → Problem Size, filled as a *paired* Actual/Desired table using the **same metric**, only the value differing (e.g., 20→2 hrs/week; steps 8→4).
- **Two-level, sourced problem sizing.** Compute cost per individual, then scale to the population, and express every size as an **explicit, auditable formula** with a cited source per factor (one exemplar: "91.4M × 30% × $500 = $13.7B/yr"). Self-made estimates are *labeled* as such.
- **Root-cause discipline.** A real 5 Whys (or fishbone) that bottoms out in an *actionable* root cause (one exemplar: "too many separate apps"), and the solution later addresses that exact cause.
- **A journey map that agrees with the JTBD**, is a visualization, carries the Causes layer, and pins causes to stages.
- **Clean, distinct use cases** and a crisp 2–4 word Problem Category drawn from the outcome statements.
- **A 4-element, solution-free Problem Statement** whose numbers match the sections above.

**What "weak" looks like (recurring failure modes):**
- **Guessed Actor counts** ("assuming from personal experience") behind precise-looking totals; **unit-labeling errors** carried through the math ("52 days" for 52 weeks); **implausible headline numbers** that don't reconcile (a "20.5 billion hours" figure ~300× off).
- **Thin gaps** — Desired only 5–8 points above Actual — so the "worthwhile problem" isn't worthwhile; and **weak problem→solution causality** (a weak workbook whose proposed product never shows *why* it would beat competitors on the outcome it targets).
- **Vague outcomes** ("little time"), metrics with **no measurement method**, or capturing only Desired (missing the gap).
- **Dropped threads:** a metric or use case introduced then never carried forward; empty placeholder diagrams referenced as if complete.
- **Solution leakage / hype** in the Problem Statement, and numbers that **contradict** the upstream Outcomes/Sizing.
- **No customer validation** — the whole case rests on personal assumption. (Course expectation: for *learning*, own experience + cited public data + verified GenAI is enough; for a *real* product it is not — always cite, and drill down to causes with actual customers.)

**The single most important lesson:** master **Outcomes** and keep **one measurable thread** — Actor + JTBD → paired Actual/Desired metrics → gap → causes → sized in dollars/time → stated solution-free. Every strong workbook does exactly this; every weak one breaks the chain somewhere.
