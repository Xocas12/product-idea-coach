# Customer Value Space — Authoritative Coaching Guide
### An independent, paraphrased study reference distilled from the product-management method taught in CMU 17-619 Product Management Essentials I. Methodology credit: Jim Berardone. This is not official course content — it is a paraphrased study aid.

> **What this Space is.** The Customer Value Space is the fourth and final Space of the Product Idea Workbook (after Customer Problem, Market, and Solution). It answers the make-or-break question: **"Will customers find our offer compelling enough to acquire and use the product?"** You already know the *problem*, the *market*, and the *solution*. Now you prove the value math: total benefits vs. total costs, a price that captures a fair slice of value, a size for the whole opportunity (TAM), and a two-page executive brief that rolls it all up.

> **The Customer Value Proposition is the spine of the whole Space.** Every concept below feeds one master evaluation:
> *"Acquire and use our product, and the total benefits you get will exceed the total costs you incur to get those benefits."* **The greater the difference, the more compelling the offer.**

**Workbook slots this Space fills (Section 4 of the Product Idea Workbook):**
`1. Product Features and Benefits → 2. Pricing Model → 3. Customer Cost Items → 4. Customer Value Proposition Evaluation` — plus the **TAM** (deferred to the last Product Assignment) and the culminating **Product Idea Brief** (written at the very end of the course).

**The value chain of this Space (how concepts feed each other):**

```
Outcomes (from Problem Space) ─┐
Features (from Solution Space) ─┤→ Features→Benefits table ─→ Customer Value (Σ benefits, tied to the outcome gap)
                                                                     │
Customer Costs / TCO ────────────────────────────────────────────── │ ──→ Value Proposition Evaluation (Benefit-to-Cost Ratio)
Pricing Model (price is a cost item) ──────────────────────────────┘         │
                                                                             ├─→ TAM (needs the price)
                                                                             └─→ Product Idea Brief (rolls up all 17 elements)
```

## 1. Customer Value Proposition (CVP)

### (a) Definition
The Customer Value Proposition is **the offer you make to customers**. Stated in the course's exact words:

> *"Acquire and use our product and the **total benefits** you get will exceed the **total costs** you incur to get those benefits."*

It is not a slogan — it is a **claim about the difference between benefits and costs**. The bigger that gap, the more compelling the offer.

### (b) Rules / constraints the course enforces
- **The customer decides** whether the offer is compelling — take a *customer-centric* view of your "offer," not a company-centric one.
- **Costs are not just price.** Costs include price paid **plus** time and effort (and social, environmental, emotional, health costs — see §4).
- The CVP is evaluated with a **Benefit-to-Cost Ratio** against friction-based thresholds (see §6). It is *not* enough to say "benefits > costs"; the ratio must clear the bar for that purchase's friction level.
- A compelling CVP requires benefits to **greatly exceed** costs — the customer needs an *attractive return on their investment*, not a marginal one.
- Emotions, biases, habits, and psychological factors legitimately count as part of the value/cost judgment.

### (c) Template / format
> **Customer Value Proposition statement (fill-in):**
> "For **[Actor]** doing **[JTBD]**, **[Product]** delivers **[key quantified benefits]** at **[total costs incurred]**, so that **[net value / why it's worth it]**."

The workbook operationalizes the CVP through four artifacts that together *prove* the statement: the **Features→Benefits table** (benefits), **Customer Value** (Σ benefits tied to the outcome gap), **Customer Cost Items / TCO** (costs), and the **Value Proposition Evaluation** (the ratio).

### (d) Worked examples (from the decks)
- **Zoom Enterprise (mid-size consulting firm, 300 employees):** Benefits $300K/yr (travel −$150K, commute time −$100K, retention +$50K) vs. Costs $70K/yr → BCR **4.3:1** → *compelling*.
- **American Express High-Yield Savings:** low-friction, near-zero cost → BCR **3.3:1** → *compelling*.

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- Treating price as the only cost (ignoring time, effort, switching, risk).
- Writing the CVP as marketing copy ("a beacon of change," "paradigm shift") instead of a benefits-minus-costs claim — the exemplar workbooks that read as ad copy (one narrative-heavy workbook) buried their real PM reasoning.
- Asserting "compelling" without a ratio or without matching the ratio to the purchase's friction.
- Company-centric value ("our AI is cutting-edge") instead of customer-centric value ("saves you $40 per visit").

### (f) Coaching questions
1. In one sentence, what does the customer *get*, and what does it *cost them* (money **and** time/effort)?
2. Would the customer themselves describe this as clearly worth it — or only your product team?
3. What is the single biggest benefit and the single biggest cost? Does the benefit dwarf the cost?
4. What non-price costs (learning, switching, risk) might the customer feel that you haven't named?
5. If you had to defend "compelling" to a skeptical CFO, what number would you point to?

### (g) Inputs → Outputs
- **Consumes:** Outcomes (Problem Space), Features (Solution Space), Competition/Positioning (Market Space).
- **Produces:** the framing that the Features→Benefits table, Customer Value, Costs/TCO, and the Value Proposition Evaluation all serve. It is the thesis the rest of the Space defends.

## 2. Product Features → Benefits Table

### (a) Definition
A **benefit** is *a gain or advantage a customer receives from your product or feature* — it is about the **IMPACT** on the customer: the **improvement to the customer's RESULTS and EXPERIENCES** caused by the product/feature. A **feature is a source of benefits, but the feature is not the benefit.** Benefits are **gains compared to not having the feature.**

The **Features→Benefits table** connects each key feature to the benefit(s) a customer receives from it. This is the required deliverable for Product Assignment 6.

### (b) Rules / constraints
- **Feature ≠ benefit.** A feature may be beneficial, but you must state the *change in results or experiences* it causes. (Duolingo gamification — points, streaks, badges — is *beneficial*, but the benefit is *increased language skills*, not the gamification itself.)
- **Benefits are relative** — phrase them with comparison words: *More, Less, Lower, Higher, Faster, Slower, Reduced, Increased, Improved, Safer, Stronger.*
- **Aim to quantify at least one benefit.** Quantification is (1) more persuasive and (2) becomes a design/build target. In-class exercise standard: **4 features, a benefit for each, quantify ≥1.**
- **Every feature must create a customer benefit.** (FYI from the deck: 70–80% of features in a typical software product go unused — don't list features with no benefit.)
- **Include the benefits of your *differentiating* features** — those are *why customers choose you.*
- Benefits can be **quantitative** (e.g., "Reduced travel time by 30%") **or qualitative** (e.g., "Reduce payment anxiety," "More peace of mind").

### The three sources of customer benefits (Tip 4 — memorize)
Source: Sawhney, *"Fundamentals of Customer Value,"* CIO magazine, 2003.

| # | Source | What the customer gains | Examples |
|---|--------|-------------------------|----------|
| 1 | **Functional** | Doing the JTBD better | Productivity (time, output), Speed, Efficiency, Knowledge/Skills, better Decisions |
| 2 | **Economic** (a.k.a. "Business value" in B2B) | Money impact | Costs ($), Revenue ($), Profit ($), Compliance |
| 3 | **Emotional** (experience) | Better experience buying/using/owning/socializing the product | Satisfaction, Experience, Relationship, Reputation |

> **Coaching move — "So what?":** To surface a benefit from a feature, ask *"What impact will this have on the Actor? What change in results/experiences? So what?"* — repeat "So what?" until you reach the true benefit.

### (c) Template / format
> **Feature → Benefit table:**
>
> | Features | Benefits |
> |----------|----------|
> | [feature 1] | [gain, phrased relatively; quantify if possible] |
> | [feature 2] | [gain 1]; [gain 2] |

Slots: `[feature]` = a capability of the MVP; `[benefit]` = the relative change in the Actor's results/experiences, quantified where possible.

### (d) Worked examples (from the decks)
- **Google Maps (mobile):** *Turn-by-turn navigation →* "Save 10–15 min per 1-hour trip by avoiding wrong turns"; *Real-time traffic/re-routing →* "Saves 20–30 hrs/year for frequent commuters; reduces stress from delays"; *Offline maps →* "Saves $30–$60 per international trip (no roaming)."
- **Zoom:** *Screen sharing →* "Improved attendees' understanding; faster decision-making"; *Automated transcription →* "Reduced note-taking time; greatly reduced transcription costs."
- **LendingHome/Kiavi:** *Faster mortgage quoting →* "Only 60 seconds (used to take hours/days)."
- **App-deployment plug-in (anonymized exemplar):** *One-click deployment →* "Saves up to 60 minutes per app deployment."

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- Listing a feature and calling it the benefit ("gamification," "AI-powered," "blockchain").
- Benefits with no comparison word — they read as descriptions, not gains.
- Never quantifying anything (all-qualitative tables are weaker and give no design target).
- Omitting the differentiating features' benefits (then the reader can't see why they'd switch).
- Loading the table with unused/nice-to-have features that create no customer benefit.

### (f) Coaching questions
1. For each feature, what *changes* for the Actor — and change *compared to what* (the current alternative)?
2. Which of the three sources (functional / economic / emotional) does each benefit belong to — and are all three represented somewhere?
3. Which feature is your *differentiator*, and what benefit makes it worth switching for?
4. Can you attach a number to at least one benefit (time, %, $)? What data or assumption gets you there?
5. Say "so what?" three times to your weakest benefit — where do you land?

### (g) Inputs → Outputs
- **Consumes:** MVP Features (Solution Space), Outcomes (Problem Space, to anchor quantification), differentiators (Positioning).
- **Produces:** the raw list of benefits that get **summed into Customer Value (§3)** and that feed the **Value Proposition Evaluation (§6)** and the **customer-justification** in the brief.

## 3. Customer Value (Σ benefits, tied to the outcome gap)

### (a) Definition
**Customer Value** is *the customer's perceived worth of your product* — the **total benefits** the customer receives from **all** of your product's features:

> benefit 1 + benefit 2 + benefit 3 + … = **Customer Value** = the worth of your product.

### (b) Rules / constraints
- **Use your Outcomes to identify the value.** The product's value is **the amount of the outcome gap(s) you reduce or eliminate.** (Outcomes are what matters most to the customer — this is the bridge from the Problem Space.)
- Value can be expressed **in time** or **in money** (convert time to money via a compensation/wage rate when you need dollars).
- Value is *perceived* and *customer-specific*: in practice you work with the customer, using *their* data and assumptions.

### (c) Template / format
> **Value = size of the outcome gap you close.**
> Actual Outcome: **[current metric value]**
> Desired Outcome: **[target metric value]**
> If your product reduces the gap by **[amount]**, the value is **"[Save/Gain X per unit]."**
>
> **Monetary conversion:** Value ($) = (**[time saved]** ÷ 60) × **[hourly rate]** — or — **[units avoided]** × **[cost per unit]**.

### (d) Worked examples (from the decks)
- **Airline passenger, security checkpoint:** Actual 20–25 min → Desired 5 min; product cuts 15–20 min → **Value = "Save 15–20 minutes of time-in-line per trip."**
- **Rideshare rider (monetary):** Actual $25.44/trip → Desired $13.00; product cuts $12 → **Value = "Save $12.00 per trip."**
- **Urgent-care patient:** Actual 103 min/visit → Desired 30 min; product cuts 25 min → **Value = "Save 25 minutes of visit time."** (Later monetized: 73 min saved × $33/hr ÷ 60 = **$40.15/visit**.)
- **Consumer health app:** Avoid 1 urgent-care visit ($200) + reduce lab tests ($150) = **Total value $350/year.**

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- Computing value from *features* instead of from the *outcome gap* (drifts away from what the customer cares about).
- Summing benefits that don't map to any Desired Outcome — inflates value with things the customer doesn't weight.
- Double-counting overlapping benefits.
- Stating value only qualitatively when a monetary figure is available and needed (especially for pricing and for the brief's business impact).

### (f) Coaching questions
1. Which Desired Outcome gap does each benefit close, and by how much?
2. If you sum all the benefits, what's the total worth — in time and in dollars?
3. What wage/compensation rate (or cost-per-unit) converts your time savings to money, and is it defensible?
4. Which benefit is the *largest* single contributor to value — is your product's story built around it?
5. Are any benefits double-counted or not actually tied to an outcome the customer wants?

### (g) Inputs → Outputs
- **Consumes:** Features→Benefits table (§2), Actual/Desired Outcomes and the Problem's gap (Problem Space).
- **Produces:** the **total-benefit figure** that goes into **value-based pricing (§5)** and the **numerator of the Benefit-to-Cost Ratio (§6)**.

## 4. Customer Costs / Total Cost of Ownership (TCO)

### (a) Definition
**Customer Costs** are *the costs the customer incurs to get the benefits* from your product. They can be **monetary and non-monetary** — price, fees, time/effort, social, environmental, emotional, health (a.k.a. **hard vs. soft**, **direct vs. indirect** costs). The **complete set** of costs across the product's whole life with the customer is the **Total Cost of Ownership (TCO)**.

### (b) Rules / constraints
- Identify the customer's **complete** set of costs across the **four life stages**:
  1. Cost of **acquiring** the product
  2. Cost of **using** the product
  3. Cost of **having** the product
  4. Cost of **disposing** of the product
- **Time/effort costs can be converted to money** using a compensation rate (same technique as valuing time savings).
- For the assignment: you **identify** the full set of customer costs, and you **set a price** (§5), but you are **not required to estimate the other (non-price) costs in monetary terms.**
- Remember the biggest cost is not always price — for a *free* product, the largest cost may be **time/effort** (deck discussion: the largest cost of the free Duolingo plan is the user's *time*).

### The four cost categories (checklist)
| # | Category | Items |
|---|----------|-------|
| 1 | **Transaction costs** | Price paid, taxes, shipping/delivery/handling fees |
| 2 | **Other monetary costs** | Accessories/equipment, consumables (ink, fuel), utilities, repair/maintenance, insurance, updates, storage |
| 3 | **Time (effort) costs** | Time to use, install/setup, data prep/conversion, integration, learning (informal + formal), troubleshooting |
| 4 | **Social & environmental costs** | Privacy (personal info), reputation (image, peer judgment), environmental (disposal) — plus emotional/health |

### (c) Template / format
> **Customer Cost Items table (per unit / per year):**
>
> | Cost item | Life stage (acquire/use/have/dispose) | Category (1–4) | Amount (if estimated) | Frequency |
> |-----------|----------------------------------------|----------------|-----------------------|-----------|
> | [Price paid] | Acquire | 1 | $[__] | one-time |
> | [Setup/learning time] | Acquire/Use | 3 | [hrs × rate] | one-time |
> | [Subscription] | Use | 1 | $[__]/yr | recurring |
> | … | … | … | … | … |
>
> **Total = the above, annualized (per year).**

### (d) Worked examples (from the decks)
- **Smartphones (company buying N devices):** device + taxes + shipping + wireless plan/yr + apps + case + charger + repair/yr + **staff labor** (setup time for security/identity/app+data transfer, IT troubleshooting time at $/hr) → **total annualized.**
- **Driverless forklift trucks:** vehicle price + delivery + taxes + one-time setup/implementation + professional services (integration with WMS, new work-process design) + learning costs (training fees, lost productivity) + operating costs (staff support $/hr) + warranty/service plan/yr + charging station (one-time) + electricity/yr. (Classic multi-stage TCO with heavy *use/have* costs.)

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- Counting only the **price** and ignoring use/have/dispose costs (the whole point of TCO).
- Forgetting **time/effort** costs (setup, learning, integration) — often the dominant cost for software and for "free" products.
- Ignoring **switching costs** and **risk/uncertainty** as felt costs (they drive the friction level in §6).
- Omitting soft costs (privacy, reputation, disposal) that can kill adoption.
- In exemplar workbooks, folding a **complement's full cost** into your product's cost envelope (a weak exemplar counted the entire third-party IDE license) inflates the stated cost and muddies the true incremental cost.

### (f) Coaching questions
1. Walk the four life stages — acquire, use, have, dispose — what does the customer pay at each?
2. What time and effort must the customer spend to get value (setup, learning, integration, troubleshooting)?
3. What must they buy *alongside* your product (accessories, consumables, complements)?
4. Are there switching costs from what they use today? Social, privacy, or reputational costs?
5. For a free/low-price product, what is the customer's *biggest* real cost — and is it time?

### (g) Inputs → Outputs
- **Consumes:** MVP/Solution (what the customer must set up, integrate, learn), Pricing Model (price is cost item #1).
- **Produces:** the **total-cost figure** that becomes the **denominator of the Benefit-to-Cost Ratio (§6)** and the customer-cost list in the brief. Also surfaces the **friction signals** (money, time, switching, risk) that set the required BCR threshold.

## 5. Pricing Model

### (a) Definition
A **Pricing Model** *defines how a product's prices are determined and presented to customers.* It identifies: **what** the customer is charged for, **how** the price is set, **how often** they pay, and the **payment options.** (Note: price is itself a *customer cost item* — the price you set is cost item #1 in §4.)

### (b) The 4 main elements of a Pricing Model
1. **Price Setting Strategy**
2. **Pricing Metric(s)** — the unit charged for
3. **Price(s)** — the actual number(s)
4. **Payment Structure** — when & how they pay

> **Steps to build it (course does Steps 1–4; required even if price = $0):**
> 1. Select a price-setting strategy → 2. Choose pricing metric(s) → 3. Set the price(s) → 4. Choose a payment structure → *(later: 5. test, 6. finalize, 7. communicate, 8. monitor/adjust).*
> **Treat your price as a hypothesis to be tested later.**

---

### 5.1 Price-Setting Strategy (4 approaches)

| Strategy | Rule |
|----------|------|
| **Uniform** | Charge all customers the same price. |
| **Segmented** (a.k.a. tiered / Good–Better–Best) | Charge different *standard* prices to different customers. |
| **Dynamic** | Let the market set prices by supply/demand. |
| **Negotiated** | Negotiate price with each customer. |

*(2, 3, 4 are non-uniform — they accommodate different needs and willingness to pay to maximize sales/profit.)*

**Segmented pricing — Good/Better/Best (memorize this structure):**
- **Tier 1 = your CORE OFFER** — the price point where **most revenue** is expected. Also serves as an **anchor** for perceived value of the other tiers.
- **A cheaper tier for Price-Sensitive customers:** *take away* features/benefits from the core offer that price-sensitive customers don't care about.
- **A premium tier for Price-INsensitive customers:** *add* features/benefits/services the price-insensitive care about.
- Each tier satisfies customers with **similar needs but different willingness to pay**; grows sales and customer count **without a significant cost increase** to your org.

---

### 5.2 Pricing Metric(s) — the unit charged

A **pricing metric** is a **unit to which the price is applied** ("what are you charging for?" a.k.a. *pricing unit*). **Total paid rises as the customer consumes more units** (1 unit = $100, 2 = $200, 3 = $300).

**Classes of pricing metrics:**

| Class | Basis | Examples |
|-------|-------|----------|
| **Item-based** | per item | McDonald's per sandwich; Nike per pair; concert per admission |
| **User-based** | per person/seat | Verizon per line; SAP SuccessFactors per employee; Notion per seat |
| **Usage-based** | as usage rises, pay rises | Electric utility per kWh; VISA per transaction; AWS per storage; Google Maps API per request |
| **Output-based** | as output produced rises, pay rises | OpenAI per 1M tokens; Synthesia per video-minute; CaseMark per document; Kustomer per conversation |
| **Outcome-based** | as results improve, pay rises | Google Ads per click; Rolls-Royce per engine uptime-hour; Zendesk AI per successful resolution; Conversica per sales-ready lead |
| **Hybrid** | multiple metrics | Uber (per ride + per mile + per minute); Google Drive (per user + per storage tier) |

> **Outcome-based pricing is increasingly used** with genAI and AI agents.

**5 guidelines for selecting metrics:**
1. **Start with the customer value (benefits)** — pick a metric *aligned* with it (more benefit → they pay more → you get more → as the customer grows, your business grows).
2. The metric should **align with your costs** (as your cost to make/deliver rises, price rises).
3. The customer can **easily measure/count** it.
4. Your business can **easily measure and enforce** it.
5. **Keep it simple** — more than 2 metrics gets confusing and slows the sale.

> **Do not confuse Pricing Metric with Payment Frequency.** "$12 **per user** **per month**": *per user* = pricing metric (what you charge for); *per month* = billing/payment frequency (how often). Companies don't always state the metric explicitly.

---

### 5.3 Setting the Price — 3 methods

#### Method 1 — Value-Based Pricing (the course's primary method; 3 variants)

**1.1 Estimate the economic value to the customer**
1. Quantify the value the customer receives **in monetary terms** (use your quantified benefits; convert to money; in practice use the customer's data/assumptions).
2. Select a price **≈ 10–30% of that value** (customers won't pay 100% of value).
   - Consider the **probability** the customer actually gets the value (lower probability → closer to 10%).
   - **Consumer markets: 10–20%** common. **Business markets: 15–30%** common.
   - **High-risk innovation: 1/8 to 1/10** of value.

> **Worked (urgent care):** value = **$40.15/visit** savings → **price ≈ $10/visit (¼ of value).**
> **Worked (consumer health app):** total value $350/yr → apply probability 25% → $87.50 → capture 10–30% → **$8.75–$26/yr** → considering peace-of-mind, set initial price **~$26/yr.**

**1.2 Use a reference price** — find the price of the **next best alternative (competitor)**, then adjust for the value of your **positive and negative points of difference.**
- Why it works: customers have already shown willingness to pay the alternative's price, and they'll anchor on it when evaluating yours.
- **Formula:** `Value-Based Price = Reference Price + Σ(value of positive differences) − Σ(value of negative differences)`.
- **3 ways to pick a reference price** (best → worst): (1) the **next best alternative's price** (most effective, *assuming same pricing metric*); (2) a **similar product used for a different problem**; (3) an **unrelated product your users buy** (least effective, can mislead).

> **Worked (Amazon Prime Video Standalone vs. Netflix-with-Ads $7.99):** + Ad-free ($6–$10) + 4K ($4) − weaker recommendations ($13) → **≈ $8.99/member/month.**
> **Worked (MS Office 365 vs. Google Workspace $12):** − 1TB less storage (−$5) + advanced apps/integration/offline (+$5–$6) → **$12–$13/user.**
> **Worked (earthmoving vehicle):** price $74,500 captured **27% of the differentiated value.**

**1.3 Create a reference price** — when the product is **so unique there's no comparable reference.** Set a price for one customer (negotiate; don't advertise), read their reaction (no resistance → raise it next time; strong resistance → lower it), and keep adjusting until you find a price that works.

#### Method 2 — Cost-Plus (cost-based) Pricing
Take the cost to make the product and add a fixed **markup** %.
> Example: $100 cost + 50% markup = $150. Typical markups: fountain soda 300%, served coffee 80%, grocery staples 5–8% / perishables 60%, jewelry 250–300%, iPhone ~120%. Markup covers Marketing/Selling, R&D, G&A, and cost-to-make, leaving profit per unit.

#### Method 3 — Competition-Based Pricing
Set the price to **match or beat the competition.** Used when your product is very similar to another (e.g., gasoline, Coca-Cola vs. Pepsi).

---

### 5.4 Payment Structure — the 3 elements
Defines **when** and **how** a customer pays (a.k.a. payment terms), whereas the *pricing metric* defines *what* they're charged for.

1. **Frequency** — *how often is the price charged?* None (free) / one-time / monthly / yearly / other.
2. **Timing (billing period)** — *when is payment due?* At purchase / start of month / end of month / one year later / half-upfront-half-later / other.
3. **Source & Method** — *how will you accept payment* (credit card, wire, ACH…) and *who is charged* (user, organization, another organization).

> **Worked (Duolingo):** Free ($0, per member, none/none/none) · Super ($6.99, per member, per month, billed annually, auto-pay card/PayPal) · Super Family ($9.99, per family ≤6, per month, billed annually). **FREE does not mean there's no pricing metric** — each free member has a distinct account (metric = per member).

### (c) Template / format — the full Pricing Model
> | Element | Fill-in |
> |---|---|
> | **Price-setting strategy** | Uniform / Segmented (tiers) / Dynamic / Negotiated |
> | **Pricing metric(s)** | [item / user / usage / output / outcome / hybrid] |
> | **Price(s)** | $[__] per [metric] (per tier if segmented) |
> | **Payment structure** | Frequency: [__] · Timing: [__] · Source/Method + Who pays: [__] |

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- Confusing pricing metric with payment frequency ("per month" is not a metric).
- Pricing at 100% of value (customers won't pay it) — or, conversely (Toptal reading), pricing so low that **payback < 6 months**, leaving money on the table.
- Using a competitor reference price when the competitor uses a *different pricing metric* (breaks the comparison).
- Segmented tiers that aren't tied to distinct willingness-to-pay/needs, or a core tier that isn't the revenue anchor.
- Picking a metric misaligned with value or with your costs, or using >2 metrics (confusing).
- Setting price arbitrarily instead of deriving it (strong exemplars *derived* price as ¼ of estimated per-tier value, then rounded to competitive numbers with each deviation justified).

### (f) Coaching questions
1. Which strategy fits — one price for all, or tiers for different willingness to pay? What's your **core (revenue-anchor) tier**?
2. What's the natural **unit of value** to charge for (item/user/usage/output/outcome)? Does it grow as the customer gets more benefit?
3. If value-based: what's the customer's monetary value, and what % (10–30%, or 1/8–1/10 if high-risk) are you capturing — and why that %?
4. If reference-based: what's the next best alternative's price, and what are your + and − points of difference worth?
5. **Frequency, timing, source/method, who pays** — have you specified all four?
6. Is your metric distinct from your billing frequency, and can both you and the customer easily measure it?

### (g) Inputs → Outputs
- **Consumes:** Customer Value (§3, for value-based pricing), Competition/Positioning (Market Space, for reference pricing), differentiators.
- **Produces:** the **price** (cost item #1 in §4), the input the **BCR (§6)** needs, and the **average price** and **pricing metric** the **TAM (§7)** requires (the metric sets the unit the TAM counts in).

## 6. Value Proposition Evaluation

### (a) Definition
The final judgment of the Space: decide whether the CVP is **compelling** — i.e., whether the **benefits the customer gets greatly exceed the costs they incur.** Operationalized via the **Benefit-to-Cost Ratio (BCR)** measured against **friction-based thresholds.** *The customer decides* if it's compelling.

### (b) Rules / constraints — BCR thresholds by purchase friction

| Required BCR | Friction level | Characteristics |
|--------------|----------------|-----------------|
| **3:1** | **Low friction** | Minutes to decide; low money; low time; little/no switching cost |
| **4–5:1** | **Medium friction** | Days/weeks to decide; moderate money; some implementation time; switching costs |
| **6–8:1** | **High friction** | Months to decide; significant money; complex implementation; major switching costs; big consequences to company/career |
| **10:1** | **Very high friction** | High friction + high benefit uncertainty, long-term consequences, safety risks |

- **BCR = total Customer Value ÷ total Customer Costs.** The ratio must **clear the bar for its friction level** — a 4:1 offer is compelling for a low-friction buy but *not* for a very-high-friction one.
- **Emotional benefits can drive a high BCR** — customers acquire products that make them *feel better* (recognition, accomplishment, belonging, self-expression, purpose, security, reduced complexity). Look for strong signals: customers talk positively, spread the word, use emotional language.

### Levers if the CVP is NOT compelling
1. **Increase the benefits** (add features, better design/experience).
2. **Reduce the customer's costs** (lower price or other cost items).
3. **Accelerate Time-to-First-Value** (make it easier to start getting benefits).
4. **Increase the probability of getting the benefits** (training, support).
5. **Increase preference for your product** (stronger differentiation).

### The 3 risk types (risk lowers compellingness)
Risk = the *uncertainties* customers have when acquiring. Less risk → stronger value prop → easier to justify.
1. **Time-to-Value Risk** — it may take too long for benefits to exceed costs (→ churn).
2. **Value Risk** — the customer may not get the promised set of benefits.
3. **Downside Risk** — potential to lose money.

### (c) Template / format
> **Value Proposition Evaluation:**
> - Total Customer Value (benefits): **$[__] / [time]**
> - Total Customer Costs: **$[__] / [time]**
> - **Benefit-to-Cost Ratio = [__]:1**
> - Purchase friction level: **[low / medium / high / very high]** → required BCR **[3 / 4–5 / 6–8 / 10]:1**
> - Verdict: **[compelling / not compelling]**
> - If not compelling → lever(s) to apply: **[increase benefits / reduce costs / speed TTFV / raise probability / raise preference]**
> - Key risks: **[time-to-value / value / downside]**

### (d) Worked examples (from the decks)
- **Zoom (mid-size consulting, 300 emp.):** $300K benefits ÷ $70K costs = **4.3:1** → medium friction → **compelling.**
- **AmEx High-Yield Savings:** **3.3:1**, low friction → **compelling.**
- **Instagram:** benefits high (connection, self-expression, social approval), costs low (free + ad interruptions + time) → low friction → **compelling** despite some switching cost from TikTok.

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- Reporting a BCR without matching it to the purchase's friction level (a 4:1 on a very-high-friction enterprise sale is *not* compelling).
- Ignoring emotional benefits (undercounts value) or ignoring risk (overcounts compellingness).
- Declaring "compelling" with no ratio and no numbers.
- When not compelling, having no plan — strong workbooks name the specific lever(s) to pull.
- Inflating the numerator with time valued at implausibly high wage rates (a weak exemplar hit 26:1–31:1 — a gap so large it reads as overstated willingness-to-pay rather than insight).

### (f) Coaching questions
1. What's your BCR (total value ÷ total cost), and in what units?
2. How much *friction* is this purchase — minutes or months to decide, how much money, how big the switching cost and risk? Which threshold applies?
3. Does your BCR clear that threshold? If not, which of the 5 levers moves it the most?
4. Which of the 3 risks (time-to-value, value, downside) is highest, and how do you reduce it?
5. Are you counting emotional benefits — and do you have signals (word-of-mouth, emotional language) that they're real?

### (g) Inputs → Outputs
- **Consumes:** Customer Value (§3), Customer Costs/TCO (§4), Price (§5), friction signals (from cost/switching/risk analysis).
- **Produces:** the **compellingness verdict** and the **"why customers will find it compelling"** element of the Product Idea Brief; if not compelling, the levers become MVP/pricing revisions.

## 7. Total Addressable Market (TAM)

### (a) Definition
The **TAM** (a.k.a. Total Available Market) is *the overall sales opportunity available to your product **IF every customer in your market acquired it** (i.e., 100% market share).* Your **Market** = everyone who matches your Actor and is doing the JTBD. TAM helps executives and investors decide if the market has **enough potential.**

### (b) Rules / constraints
- **TAM is deferred:** *"Do NOT complete this part until the last Product Assignment of this course."*
- **TAM is measured 2 ways:** (1) potential **number of units sold per year**; (2) potential **revenue per year** (in currency).
- **TAM is always annual.**
- **Your TAM ($) is not the same as a competitor's** unless it's the same price aimed at the same market (TAM is price-dependent).
- Requires your **pricing model** — you can't compute the monetary TAM without a price.
- **Count in units of the pricing metric, not in people.** The Market Space counts Actors, and Actors are people. The price is charged per unit of the pricing metric (§5.2), and that unit is often not one Actor: per family or household, per organization or site, per conversation. Convert before you multiply: **purchasing units = no. Actors × pricing-metric units per Actor.**
  - **Per-user / per-member pricing where the Actor holds the account:** units per Actor = **1**, so purchasing units = Actors.
  - **Per family, household, organization or site:** units per Actor = **1 ÷ (avg Actors per unit)**, a fraction. 12M parents at 1.6 parents per household = 7.5M households.
  - **Usage, output or outcome metrics:** units per Actor = the metric's **usage per Actor per year** (Agentforce below: 20 × 260 = 5,200 conversations per rep).
  - **Per seat bought by the Actor for other people** (a manager buying seats for a team): units per Actor = avg seats bought per Actor, which can be more than 1.
  - State the factor and its source next to the TAM. It is the only allowed difference between the Market Space count and the TAM count.

### (c) Template / format — the two formulas
> **Monetary TAM:**
> `TAM ($) = (no. purchasing units in your market) × (avg price per unit of the pricing metric) × (avg no. purchases/year per unit)`
>
> **Units TAM:**
> `TAM (units) = (no. purchasing units in your market) × (no. purchases per unit in one year)`
>
> where `no. purchasing units = (no. Actors in your market, from the Market Space) × (pricing-metric units per Actor)`.
>
> "Purchases/year" is how many times the stated price is paid for one unit in a year (12 for a price quoted per month, 1 for a yearly price or a one-time purchase). When the price is per user and the Actor is the user, units per Actor = 1 and the formula is the course's `Actors × avg price × purchases/year`.
>
> **Under a segmented (tiered) pricing strategy**, use a *good-enough* price:
> **(1) use the middle price**, **OR (2) calculate the average price** across tiers.
> **Convert every tier to the same pricing metric first.** A per-family tier mixed into per-member tiers is not averageable as listed: divide the family price by the average Actors on a family plan. Duolingo (§5.4): Free $0 and Super $6.99 are per member, Super Family $9.99 is per family of up to 6. At a full family of six that is $9.99 ÷ 6 = $1.67 per member, so the average is (0 + 6.99 + 1.67) ÷ 3 = **$2.89 per member per month**. Averaging the listed prices, (0 + 6.99 + 9.99) ÷ 3 = $5.66, is about 2x high. The middle tier (Super, $6.99 per member) is already per member and needs no conversion.

### (d) Worked examples (from the decks)
- **College students:** 207M students × $100 × 1 purchase/yr = **$20.7B/yr revenue = 207M units/yr.**
- **Salesforce Agentforce:** 2.9M US customer-service reps × 20 conversations/rep/day × 260 workdays = 15.1B conversations/yr × $2.00 = **$30.2B/yr = 15.1B conversations/yr.** The pricing metric is per conversation, so 20 × 260 = 5,200 conversations per rep per year is the units-per-Actor conversion, and each conversation is paid for once.
- **Per household (illustrative numbers):** Actor = *Parents of toddlers*; the Market Space counts **12M parents**. The product costs **$8 per household per month**, and one plan covers both parents; households in the market average **1.6 parents** who are Actors (cite the household survey or census table you used). Purchasing units = 12M × (1 ÷ 1.6) = **7.5M households**. TAM ($) = 7.5M × $8 × 12 = **$720M/yr**; TAM (units) = **7.5M household plans/yr**. The unconverted 12M parents × $8 × 12 = $1.15B counts each household's plan once per parent and is 1.6x high.
- **Per organization (illustrative numbers):** Actor = *Warehouse shift supervisor*; the Market Space counts **400K supervisors**. The product costs **$3,000 per warehouse site per year**, and sites average **8 supervisors**. Purchasing units = 400K ÷ 8 = **50K sites**. TAM ($) = 50K × $3,000 × 1 = **$150M/yr**; TAM (units) = **50K site licenses/yr**. The unconverted 400K × $3,000 = $1.2B is 8x high, off by the head count per site.

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- Computing TAM before the last assignment (against the explicit rule).
- **Confusing TAM with a revenue forecast** — TAM assumes 100% market share; applying adoption %, conversion %, or market-share % turns it into SAM/SOM, not TAM. (Exemplar error: a weak exemplar multiplied the *entire* ~21.7M TAM by tier-conversion %, producing an unrealistic multi-billion-dollar "TAM" that was really a forecast; another labeled a 20%-adoption × 50%-conversion figure "TAM" when it's really SOM.)
- Forgetting TAM is **annual**, or using a per-week/other period (a weak exemplar reported a "per week" TAM).
- Using a price in the TAM that **contradicts the actual pricing tiers** (a weak exemplar's TAM used $10/yr while the product priced at $20/yr).
- Not reconciling the customer count across sections (market-size figures drifting between sections is the single most common exemplar weakness). The Actor count in the TAM is the Market Space count; the purchasing-unit count may differ from it only by the stated units-per-Actor factor.
- **Multiplying the Actor count by a price set per family, household or organization** — this overstates TAM by the number of Actors per unit (up to 6x for a family plan of six, 8x for a site with 8 Actors). The reverse slip, Actors × a per-seat price when each Actor buys several seats, understates it. Convert Actors to pricing-metric units first.
- For segmented pricing, using an arbitrary tier price instead of the middle or average price, or averaging tiers priced on **different metrics** (per member and per family) without converting them to one metric.

### (f) Coaching questions
1. How many Actors are in your market (doing the JTBD), and what's your source for that number?
2. What's your pricing metric, and how many pricing-metric units does one Actor account for — 1 per user, 1 ÷ (Actors per family/household/organization), or a yearly usage count? What's the source for that factor?
3. What's the average price per unit (middle or average tier if segmented, with every tier converted to the same metric), and how many purchases per unit per year?
4. Compute TAM both ways — units/year and revenue/year. Do they reconcile with your pricing model?
5. Are you accidentally applying adoption/market-share % (that's SAM/SOM, not TAM)?
6. Does the Actor count here match the numbers you used in the Market Space and problem sizing, and does the purchasing-unit count follow from it through the stated units-per-Actor factor and nothing else?

### (g) Inputs → Outputs
- **Consumes:** Market size / no. of Actors (Market Space), average price and pricing metric (Pricing Model §5), and the units-per-Actor factor that converts one into the other.
- **Produces:** the **Market Opportunity (TAM)** element of the Product Idea Brief; the headline "is this big enough?" number for executives/investors.

## 8. Product Idea Brief

### (a) Definition
A **short, structured document that summarizes a new product concept**, used as an **early-stage proposal** to decide whether it's worth exploring further — **before** significant time/resources are invested. It builds a **shared understanding** across executives and the product team. **It is written at the end of the course** (it rolls up all four Spaces). It is **not** a showcase of your analysis, features, or technology.

### (b) Rules / constraints
- **7 parts total; the course/final assignment focuses on the first 4:**
  1. **Problem** · 2. **Proposed Solution** · 3. **Market Opportunity** · 4. **Value Proposition** · *(5. Business Impact · 6. Key Risks/Unknowns · 7. Next Steps).*
- **Prose rules (strict):**
  - Written as **prose in clear, active language** — simple sentences. **NOT a list of items, and NO visuals.**
  - **No product-management jargon** (don't write "Actor," "JTBD") — **except TAM and Outcomes**, which are widely used business terms.
  - **Concise: up to 2 pages** for the assignment.
  - **Consider the audience — write for executives.** **Numbers matter** (TAM, problem size, outcomes).

### The 17 essential elements (map to the first 4 parts)
| Part | Elements |
|------|----------|
| **Problem** | Actor · JTBD · Gap · Cause(s) · Problem Size |
| **Proposed Solution** | Product name · Product category · Main Attributes (of MVP) · Software and/or Physical Deliverables · How It Solves the Problem |
| **Market Opportunity** | TAM size · Market Focus for MVP · Key Competitor · Position-to-Own |
| **Value Proposition** | Customer benefits · Pricing · Why customers will find it compelling |

*(These are exactly the artifacts you've already produced across the four Spaces — the brief assembles them into prose. Current-state and future-state framing help tell the story.)*

### (c) Template / format
> **Opening paragraph pattern (active language) — from the deck:**
> *"The purpose of this brief is to propose **[Product name]**, a new **[product category/what it is]** that **[core benefit / what it automates or improves]**, with the goal of securing executive support to pursue discovery and validation."*
>
> **Body (prose, ~2 pages, executive audience, no jargon, no visuals, numbers matter):**
> - **Problem:** who has it, the job they're doing, the gap, the cause(s), and how big it is (problem size).
> - **Proposed Solution:** the named product, its category, its main MVP attributes, what's delivered (software/physical), and how it closes the gap.
> - **Market Opportunity:** the TAM, the MVP's market focus, the key competitor, and the position you'll own.
> - **Value Proposition:** the customer benefits, the price, and why the benefits-vs-costs make it compelling.

### (d) Worked examples (from the deck)
Three sample opening paragraphs for **"PM Genie, an AI-powered copilot for product managers"** — each states the product, what it is/does, and the ask ("secure executive support," "pursue discovery and validation," "explore its feasibility") in one or two clean active sentences.

### (e) Common mistakes / anti-patterns
- Writing it **early** instead of at the end of the course (it's a roll-up of all four Spaces).
- Using **bullet lists or visuals** (violates prose rule) or **PM jargon** ("Actor," "JTBD").
- Exceeding **2 pages** or writing for the wrong audience (not executives).
- Showcasing analysis/technology instead of the decision-relevant story.
- **Omitting the numbers** executives care about (TAM, problem size, outcomes).
- Overwritten marketing prose ("beacon of change," "paradigm shift") that buries the substance — the same failure flagged in the weaker exemplar narratives.

### (f) Coaching questions
1. Can you state the product and the ask in one active opening sentence (name + what it is + "secure executive support to validate")?
2. Are all 17 elements present across the four parts — Problem, Solution, Market Opportunity, Value Proposition?
3. Have you removed every PM jargon word (except TAM/outcomes) and every bullet/visual?
4. Is it ≤2 pages and written for an executive who cares about numbers?
5. Which three numbers (TAM, problem size, key outcome) anchor the brief — are they in it and consistent with the rest of the workbook?

### (g) Inputs → Outputs
- **Consumes:** every prior Space — Problem (Actor/JTBD/Gap/Causes/Size), Solution (name/category/attributes/deliverables/how-it-solves), Market (TAM/Focus/Competitor/Position-to-Own), Value (benefits/pricing/compellingness).
- **Produces:** the culminating executive deliverable — the go/no-go proposal that decides whether the idea is worth pursuing.

## Strong vs. Weak — grounded in anonymized exemplar workbooks

**What separates a strong Customer Value Space from a weak one** (patterns across a set of completed workbooks):

**Strong (do this):**
- **Golden-thread consistency.** The best workbooks pick 2–3 core numbers early (e.g., 8→4 steps, $32/hr wage, 40 units/yr) and **reuse them verbatim** across benefits, value, pricing, and TAM so the whole Space reconciles as one argument.
- **Benefits tied to the outcome gap and quantified in both time and dollars,** using **conservative, sourced assumptions** (a strong exemplar carried one dollar figure — $1,664/yr and 68 hrs saved — verbatim, explicitly lower-bound → credible ROI).
- **Value-based pricing done properly:** estimate per-tier perceived value, capture ~¼ (within the 10–30% rule), round to competitive prices, and **justify every deviation** (several strong exemplars).
- **Costs treated as full TCO,** including time/effort and switching, with time monetized (a strong exemplar costed 115 min of onboarding at $25/hr = $47.92).
- **Segmented tiers mapped 1:1 to willingness-to-pay,** each tier's contents justified (a strong exemplar used free-for-students / premium-for-workers / live-expert-for-elderly tiers).
- **A BCR (or explicit value ratio) matched to friction,** with named levers if weak.
- **A TAM stated as an explicit annual formula** (`purchasing units × price × purchases/yr`, with purchasing units = Actors × units per Actor), with segment sizes reconciling to the market total.

**Weak (avoid this):**
- **Numbers that drift between sections** — market/customer counts and prices that don't reconcile (one weak exemplar cited 60M vs. 150M and a "per week" TAM; another carried inconsistent 2M/2.5M/3M audience counts across sections). This is the single most common flaw.
- **TAM confused with a forecast** — multiplying the whole market by adoption/conversion % and calling it TAM (a weak exemplar's multi-billion-dollar figure; another's SOM-labeled-TAM).
- **Guessed inputs dressed as precision** — segment sizes "assumed from personal experience" with no research, or load-bearing benefit values (e.g., "50% of deployments fail") asserted without evidence (several weak exemplars).
- **Value asserted, not proven** — no side-by-side price-vs-value benchmark against named competitors (one weak exemplar), or value ratios so inflated (26:1–31:1) they read as overstated willingness-to-pay (another weak exemplar).
- **Costs = price only,** ignoring time/switching, or folding a complement's full cost into the envelope (a weak exemplar included a whole third-party IDE license).
- **A brief (or narrative) written as marketing hype** ("beacon of innovation," "paradigm shift") instead of concrete, numbered, executive-ready prose.

**The one-line test:** a strong Customer Value Space lets a skeptical executive trace a single, consistent set of numbers from the *outcome gap* → *quantified benefits* → *value* → *price (≈10–30% of value)* → *BCR that clears its friction threshold* → *annual TAM* → *a two-page jargon-free brief* — with every figure reconciling to the ones before it.
